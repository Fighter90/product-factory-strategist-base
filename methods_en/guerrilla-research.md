# Guerrilla research — fast, cheap, without a full sample

## When to use
A quick answer to a specific question is needed (today–tomorrow), and there is no budget or time for a full study: testing a problem statement, screening hypotheses before design, getting a first signal about demand, quickly testing a prototype. This card covers how to run such research to get a signal — and how not to present the signal as proof.

## Core idea
**Guerrilla research** is data collection on a convenience (non-representative) sample in hours–days and for minimal money: short interviews with passers-by/colleagues/users at hand, an express survey of 10–25 people, a test on whatever traffic is available. The price of speed is **sampling bias**: whoever happened to be at hand is not the target audience. Therefore: a signal — yes; prevalence and effect size — no.

**Where it is acceptable:**
- early stages: the "diverging" half of Double Diamond, hypothesis screening, problem wording;
- testing wording and prototypes: did the person understand the text, can they complete the scenario ([[M-usability-testing]]);
- direction finding: "the pain seems real — a proper study is worth it".

**Where it is unacceptable:** a decision to roll out to the whole base, market size and share estimates, conversion "as in real life", monetization decisions with irreversible consequences. For those — representative samples and experiments ([[M-quant-methods]], [[M-ab-testing]]).

**Format and volume:** express interview 5–10 minutes from a script of 5–7 questions; survey — 8–12 questions, 10–25 respondents; usability — 5 people per round. More respondents on a convenience sample adds no validity — it adds deceptive confidence. Two different "guerrilla" runs (different channels, different people) beat one big one.

**The speed–cost–validity triad:** the guerrilla method maximizes the first two and gives up the third. The correct reading of the result is "the hypothesis was not refuted, the signal points to X" — worth its limitations; the wrong one ("60% of respondents said yes, so 60% of the market") is the method's main harm.

## Procedure
1. **One hypothesis — one question.** State what you are testing and which observation would refute it ([[M-hypothesis-testing]]). Guerrilla research answers 1–2 questions, not "everything about the product".
2. **Define the target trait of the respondent** at least roughly: who fits the hypothesis by job (a parent, a driver, an accountant), and where such people are at hand: a cafe, colleagues from other departments, a topical chat, existing users in support.
3. **Write the script:** 5–7 open questions about past experience and facts ("the last time you…", "how do you solve this now, how much do you spend"), no leading questions and no "would you buy". End with one or two closed questions for the counter.
4. **Collect 10–25 answers fast:** mix channels (offline + chat + support), record refusals and "does not fit the trait" — they are part of the picture. Do not cherry-pick the pleasant ones: rotate types of people.
5. **Collect raw data in parallel:** verbatim quotes, behavior (what they did, where they stumbled), refusals. Summarize as you go — so that at interview 15 you notice saturation and adjust questions.
6. **Summarize the signal, not statistics:** recurring vs absent themes; closed-question counter — explicitly labeled "convenience sample, n=18"; respondent types; surprises; what was refuted.
7. **Assign the evidence strength honestly:** "a weak signal on a convenience sample" ([[M-evidence-design]]). The conclusion is one of: the hypothesis is refuted (often the most valuable outcome), a signal in favor — needs stronger validation, more questions than answers.
8. **Set the next step:** a proper survey ([[M-quant-methods]]), in-depth interviews ([[M-in-depth-interviews]]), an A/B ([[M-ab-testing]]) — or closing the topic.

## Typical mistakes
- Generalizing to the market: "60% of 15 respondents" ≠ 60% of the audience.
- Leading questions: "it must be inconvenient when…" — collecting politeness, not data.
- Surveying 12 colleagues from one department and calling them "users".
- Topping up the sample with people "similar to those already surveyed" — comfort instead of data.
- Using a convenience-sample signal as a rollout criterion.
- One big survey instead of two small ones on different channels: the second bias is cheaper than the first.

## Questions that can change the decision
- Who in the target audience is physically absent near this collection channel?
- Which answers are explained by the place and circumstances of the survey itself?
- Did anything in the data refute the hypothesis — or did we read only confirmations?
- What would have to be true for the convenience sample to be representative anyway?
- How much does a proper check of this signal cost — and is the signal worth that money?
