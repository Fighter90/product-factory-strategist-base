# Competitor teardown — a deep dive into product, mechanics, and economics

## When to use
The market map is already collected ([[M-competitors]]): you know who is on the market. Now you need a deep analysis of one or two key competitors: how their product is built, why users choose them, how they make money and what drives their growth — to answer "how will we be different" and "what can we adopt". This card is about depth on one player, not breadth across all.

## Core idea
**The goal of a teardown is not "to copy features" but to understand the competitor's winning model:** what brings them users, retains them, and earns money. A torn-down competitor answers three questions: 1) where they are strong in the eyes of the shared user (a pain they solve better than we do); 2) which mechanics drive their growth (channels, product, economics); 3) which weaknesses we can exploit in positioning.

**What is analyzed (order matters — from the outside in):**
| Layer | What we look at | How |
|---|---|---|
| Positioning | to whom and what they promise: website, store listings, communication | a "blind" run: how the product looks to a new user |
| Value | which jobs they cover (JTBD), the key promise, USP | reading materials + interviews with their users |
| Product | key scenarios, onboarding, killer features, UX decisions | walking the scenario ourselves, [[M-dogfooding]] someone else's product |
| User economics | pricing tiers, monetization mechanics, "free vs paid", limits | a full walkthrough to payment, an attempt to pay |
| Growth channels | where users come from: SEO, referrals, paid traffic, partnerships | presence analysis, traffic estimates, watching promotions |
| Company economics | how they earn, revenue structure, traces of unit economics | reports (if public), job boards (who they hire = what they build), press releases |

**Dealing with competitors (not only war):** relationship options — direct competition, segmentation (agreeing on different niches), partnership/API integration, **coopetition** (competing and cooperating at once: shared standards, trend exchange via industry associations, joint infrastructure solutions). Choosing the relationship is a separate strategic decision: a "fire all ammunition" race often benefits only the third player.

**Limits of the method:** from the outside you see traces, not metrics. Estimates of a competitor's revenue/users are forecasts with assumptions — present them as estimates with a range and source, not as facts ([[M-source-criticism]]). Data from "hearsay" and pitches by former employees is testimonial, weak.

**The teardown output:** not a presentation "here are their features" but a decision: what we do differently (differentiation — [[M-usp]]), what we adopt as the market standard (user expectations), what we consciously do not copy (not our model).

## Procedure
1. **Choose 1–2 competitors for depth** from the market map: direct ones with an overlapping core job; for each — the purpose of the teardown ("why we lose users to them" / "how they entered the market").
2. **Positioning from the outside:** collect all public promises (website, stores, social media, PR); write out: target segment, key promise, proof of the promise. Compare with ours ([[M-usp]]).
3. **Walk the product as a user:** the full newcomer path: registration, onboarding, the key value action, a payment attempt, cancellation. Record: where they are faster/better than us, where worse; screenshots and numbers (number of steps, time).
4. **Dissect retention mechanics:** what makes users return (notifications, accumulated state, loyalty programs); which free-tier limits exist and how the upgrade pitch sounds ([[M-monetization]]).
5. **Estimate growth channels:** where they have presence (search, content, partnerships); signs of paid traffic (promo landing pages); referral mechanics. Estimates — with a range.
6. **Collect the external economics:** public reports/press releases, job postings (who and where they hire), store ratings and reviews (complaints = weaknesses; praise = strengths in users' own words).
7. **Interview the competitor's users** (if accessible): why they chose them, what irritates them, what retains them, whether they tried us and why they left/returned. This is the strongest source for the "value" layer.
8. **Assemble the winning model:** what drives their growth (product/channel/economics/brand); where they are vulnerable; which of their decisions are the market standard (we must match) and which are deliberate choices of their model (we are not bound).
9. **Translate into decisions:** a list: copy / counter / ignore; each justified through our model ([[M-competitors]] — update the shared market map with the findings).
10. **Set monitoring:** repeat the external layers quarterly (positioning, pricing, channels) — competitors change; assign an owner.

## Quality criteria
- The teardown goal is tied to a decision ("why we lose users to them"), not "study the competitor".
- The competitor's full user journey is walked and recorded: where they are better or worse than us, with numbers (steps, time).
- The winning model is stated: product / channel / economics / brand — what drives their growth and where they are vulnerable.
- Revenue and user-base estimates carry a range and a source and are separated from facts; hearsay is marked as testimonial.
- The competitor's user reviews are analyzed: pains become differentiation candidates.
- Every finding is converted into a decision: copy / counter / ignore — justified through our own model; monitoring is scheduled.

## Typical mistakes
- A teardown for its own sake: screenshots collected, but no decision on "what we do differently".
- Copying features without the model: a feature works in their economics but not in ours.
- Taking marketing promises for the real product (they promise X, the product has Y).
- Presenting revenue/base estimates of the competitor as facts without a range and source.
- Ignoring the competitor's user reviews — a ready list of pains for differentiation.
- One teardown forever: the market changed, the map is a year stale.

## Questions that can change the decision
- What exactly drives their growth: product, channel, economics, or brand?
- Which of their features is the market standard (without it we are not shortlisted), and which is a trap for someone else's model?
- What do their unhappy users say — and why are they still there?
- Which relationship with the competitor beats war: segmentation, integration, coopetition?
- What happens if the competitor copies our twist — will they do it faster than we find a new one?
